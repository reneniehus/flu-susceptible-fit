ECDC respiratory-virus modelling · independent analysis

What do national focal points get from ECDC forecasting — and what do the hubs actually deliver?

Two views of the same system. Demand: a survey of 19 EU/EEA National Focal Points on their in-house modelling capacity and the value they place on ECDC's short-term forecasts (RespiCast) and scenarios (RespiCompass). Delivery: five European forecasting hubs reconstructed submission-by-submission — the archived EU COVID-19 hub (2021) and its RespiCast successors — to trace which indicators were forecast, in which weeks, by how many models. The through-line: COVID-19 hospitalisation forecasts, produced almost every week since mid-2021.

Part I

Demand — the value of forecasting to national focal points

The 2024 ECDC survey (Respiratory Viruses & Legionella group and the Modelling team) went to National Focal Points (NFPs) for viral respiratory diseases — one collective response per country. It asked how much modelling they can do themselves, how aware they are of each ECDC modelling output, which decisions those outputs might inform, and which ECDC outputs they would prefer — including the two flagship products, forecasts (RespiCast) vs scenarios (RespiCompass).

Most focal points cannot model in-house — so ECDC is often the only forecast they have

Q6 · Mathematical-modelling staff in the national institute, irrespective of disease area. Each block is one of the 19 respondents.

Awareness of ECDC modelling outputs

Q5 · "How aware are you of respiratory virus burden modelling work done at ECDC?" — rated 0 (not aware) → 5 (fully aware) on a slider for each output. This is awareness, not use. It tracks how long an output has been in circulation: guidance (since 2020) is best known; the newer hubs — RespiCast (2023), RespiCompass (2024) — least. So the low scores for the newer products reflect recency and communication, not a verdict on their value.

Which output will be most valuable?

Q8 · RespiCast (weekly short-term forecasts) vs RespiCompass (seasonal scenarios). Most expect both to be useful; when forced to pick one, forecasts win by a wide margin. This is stated expectation — many respondents note they have not yet used the outputs.

Awareness by capacity: an awareness gap where the need is greatest

Q5 × Q6 · Mean awareness of each output class, by in-house modelling staff. The institutes with no team of their own — the majority, and those who would gain most — are no more aware of ECDC forecasting than better-resourced ones, and every institute entirely unaware of RespiCast is one with no team. So the awareness gap is concentrated exactly where capacity is thin — cheap to close. (Awareness is not use; the >10-staff band is a single institute — read it lightly.)

Which decisions would modelling inform?

Q7 · Likelihood that ECDC modelling informs each national action, ordered by share "likely". Situational awareness (surveillance, 58%) leads — but the canonical forecasting use case, healthcare-capacity planning, is the least likely (16%). Value concentrates in awareness, not high-stakes resource decisions.

Is there a mechanism to use the modelling?

Q10 · "There is a clear, consistent mechanism for integrating modelling outputs into public-health decisions at my institute." Only about half agree — a ceiling on realised value.

How focal points want forecasts delivered

Q11 / Q12 · Preferred communication channels, ranked (1 = most preferred). Mean rank across respondents — lower is better. A dedicated website and a newsletter beat meetings for both weekly forecasts and seasonal scenarios.

Voices from the field

Open responses (Q8b, Q13), independently theme-coded. What NFPs say about value, barriers and needs.

Where demand meets delivery

The survey measures appetite. The hubs are the meal actually being served.

Focal points told us short-term forecasting is what they most expect to value — yet even the countries with no modelling of their own are only moderately aware of the outputs, and only about half report a clear way to use modelling at all. One explanation would be that the forecasts simply aren't there for them. So the natural next question is one of supply: going back to 2021 — across the archived EU COVID-19 hub and the RespiCast successors — what did ECDC actually produce, for which indicators, in which weeks, and with how many models? If supply turns out to be durable, then the gap between value and use lives on the demand side, not the supply side.

Part II

Reconstructed from every submission in five hubs: the archived EU COVID-19 Forecast Hub (cases, hospitalisations, deaths; 2021–2024) and its RespiCast successors — RespiCast-Covid19 (hospitalisations) and RespiCast-SyndromicIndicators (ILI, ARI) — plus the archived flu and ARI hubs that ran the 2023/24 syndromic season. What this is

An independent analysis, not an ECDC publication. Built from a de-identified NFP survey export and five public European forecasting-hub repositories (the archived EU COVID-19 hub, its RespiCast successors, and the archived flu/ARI hubs). Every number here is regenerated from source by code/00_main.R; the charts read one embedded JSON produced by that pipeline.

Read carefully

19 respondents (of ~30 EU/EEA NFPs): every share moves ~5 points per person, so treat all percentages as indicative and read counts. Q5's 0–5 slider measures self-rated awareness of each output ("How aware are you of respiratory virus burden modelling work done at ECDC?") — not use. Q8 (which output would be most useful) is a stated expectation, not observed use. Newer products have had less time in circulation. Coverage counts a submission's presence, not its skill; a country appearing in a panel does not mean its NFP uses the output; one collective institutional reply per country, and NFP awareness may understate national involvement where in-house teams already submit to the hubs.
