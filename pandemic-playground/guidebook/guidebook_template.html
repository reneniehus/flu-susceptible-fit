Reading an
Outbreak

A staged field guide to the analytical toolkit — from the first signal abroad to next season's scenarios. Every answer here is checked against a truth the analyst never sees.

The premise

One outbreak, kept in two records.

This whole guide runs on one simulated pandemic: a novel respiratory pathogen emerges in a non‑EU/EEA source region X and travels by air into every EU/EEA country. The simulator keeps two parallel records — and the gap between them is the entire job of the toolkit.

The truth

What actually happened

Every infection, the real reproduction number, the true fatality rate, who imported what and when. Complete and exact — and, in a real emergency, entirely invisible.

The observed

What surveillance sees

A late, partial, noisy shadow: cases only once tested and reported, deaths that lag by weeks, imports caught only where surveillance is sharp. This is all the analyst gets.

Because the truth is known, each tool below is scored — the estimate is placed next to the answer. That is what makes this a testbed and not a demo.

Knowing when to stop believing the number

Not every question a model can be asked is one it can answer.

Every estimate here was scored against the answer, and the recurring lesson is that a method can be exactly right in principle yet biased in practice — scaling‑up testing inflates early growth, a variant contaminates R, a fixed‑R forecast overshoots the turnover. But there is a deeper gradient underneath: the tools span a spectrum from questions that are genuinely well‑posed to ones that are ill‑posed as prediction no matter how clean the code.

Well‑posed · short horizon

Recovers what is in the data

variant selection · nowcasting · near‑term Rₜ

A selection coefficient is identifiable from sequence counts; a nowcast is a bounded deconvolution of a delay we can estimate. Trust it, score it, correct its biases.

Fragile · medium horizon

The capacity forecast

phase2_forecast · 1–2 weeks

Sound in clear growth or decline — but with no behavioural feedback it can send admissions straight through capacity, which real systems never do. Past the envelope flag, read risk, not a number.

Ill‑posed as prediction · long horizon

Next season, with boosters

phase3_scenarios · a season out

Behaviour, the next variant and waning are all assumed, not measured, and they swamp the mechanism. Legitimate only to rank levers — never to quote an absolute number.

This is where foundation models enter — and where the popular framing goes wrong. A mechanistic model is overconfident outside its envelope; a data‑driven model, grounded in how real curves have actually bent, is more honest exactly there — but is itself overconfident inside its training distribution and blind when the new pathogen is unlike anything it has seen. "Antidote" is the wrong metaphor: the two fail in opposite regimes. The real prize is not a better point forecast but honest out‑of‑distribution detection — a model that can say this is unlike what I was built on. That is why the tools here carry explicit envelope flags rather than pretending a projection is always a prediction.

And this outbreak is only one the framework can stage: dial the generation interval down for a fast pandemic influenza, up for a measles‑like pathogen, or set the offspring dispersion low — as the cluster panel showed — for a SARS‑style superspreader where most imported chains simply fizzle out. The tools re‑scale to each.
